## How these were rendered

`en_US-libritts-high`, `speaker_id=42`, tone A (`length_scale` 1.00,
`noise_scale` 0.75, `noise_w_scale` 0.85), matching the approved sample
`assets/samples/sample_C_libritts_sp42.wav`.

Rendered **per beat**, not as one take, so each line lands on its scripted
timestamp. Piper varies about 11% in duration between runs on identical text and
`SynthesisConfig` exposes no seed, so every beat was synthesised 12 times and the
shortest take kept. Nothing was time-stretched or resampled to fit.

Two lines were shortened because the untrimmed read ran past the end of its
video. Both trims are recorded in the delivery report; the source scripts in
`docs/growth/2026-07-29-grok-sixth-engine/` still carry the original wording and

## Rights position

Every sample in these files was synthesised from oscillators and pseudo-random
noise by `scripts/compose_music.py` using numpy and the `wave` stdlib module.
There are **no samples, no loops, no stems, no presets and no AI music service**
anywhere in the chain. The generator is deterministic and seeded, so any track
can be reproduced bit-for-bit from the script alone.

This matters because the obvious shortcut was not available. Constantin's Suno
account is on the Free plan, whose terms state **"No commercial use"**, so no
Suno output — and no other third-party or AI-service track — can be used in
BrandGEO marketing, let alone in paid ads. Composing our own removes the
question entirely: BrandGEO owns the master and the composition, on every
channel, in perpetuity.

On the line between idiom and infringement: genre conventions are not
copyrightable. Tempo, key, instrument palette, a sidechained pad, the general
sound of a product-launch bed — all free to use. Specific melodies, basslines
and hooks are not. Every chord progression here is ordinary diatonic motion,
and every melodic figure is generated at render time from the chord tones of the
bar it sits in by the step-patterns in `TRACKS`. Nothing was transcribed from,
or modelled on, any identifiable recording.

## Measured, not asserted

Nobody has listened to these files. Everything below was measured.

| Track | I (LUFS) | TP (dBTP) | LRA (LU) | corr(L,R) | side/mid | loop seam vs local p95 step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `tension-minor` | -16.00 | -3.60 | 6.80 | 0.859 | -11.2 dB | 0.918 |
| `build-resolve` | -16.00 | -3.39 | 4.20 | 0.885 | -12.2 dB | 0.685 |
| `clean-utility` | -16.00 | -3.65 | 4.00 | 0.818 | -10.0 dB | 0.207 |
| `contrarian-drive` | -16.00 | -4.35 | 4.30 | 0.909 | -13.2 dB | 0.067 |

Loudness was set by a two-pass `loudnorm`: pass one measures, pass two applies
the resulting gain as a single scalar. The gain is deliberately linear rather
than `loudnorm`'s dynamic mode, because dynamic mode re-shapes the envelope and
would break the loop seam and flatten LRA.

Loop seams were checked on the real two-loop concatenation with a spectral-flux
onset detector. In all four tracks the flux at the join is **below** the 99th
percentile of flux inside the track (26.2 vs 56.2, 21.5 vs 82.5, 32.8 vs 65.1,
24.4 vs 56.4), so the wrap generates less of a transient than ordinary musical
events do. All four are exactly 2,880,000 samples.

## Known weaknesses, stated up front

- **`clean-utility` is the least dynamic of the four.** Its spectral centroid
  moves only 1.51x across the loop against 8.5x and 10.0x for the two minor-key
  tracks. That is partly the brief — it has to sit under on-screen text — but it
  is the one most likely to read as wallpaper.
- **The mix is centre-dominant.** Side-to-mid sits between -10 and -13 dB
  because sub, kick and mid-bass are mono by design. The pad, arp and hats are
  genuinely panned and every file differs between L and R on 100% of samples,
  but nobody will describe these as wide.
- **The `.m4a` files are not sample-exact gapless.** AAC adds encoder padding:
  each decodes to 2,880,512 samples, 512 more than the WAV. `ffprobe` reports
  60.000000s because the edit list trims the priming, but for a hard loop in an
  editor use the WAV.
- **Percussion is synthesised, not sampled.** The kick is a pitch-swept sine
  with a noise click and the hats are filtered noise. They will read as
  clean/electronic, not as an acoustic kit, and they are less characterful than
  a sampled drum library would be.
- **The `build-resolve` resolution is harmonic, not orchestral.** It lands on
  C major at bar 24 (exactly 48.000s, so the resolve occupies exactly the last
  12.000s) with an open filter and a sustained upper voice. It is a resolution,
  not a drop.

## How these were rendered

    python scripts/compose_music.py

Roughly 70 seconds for all four on this machine. `--only <name>` renders one
track; `--verify` re-measures existing files without re-rendering. The full
measurement dump is written to `assets/audio/music/_render_report.json`.

Techniques worth knowing before editing the script:

- **Musical time, not wall-clock.** Every event index is derived from bars and
  sixteenths. Both tempos divide 60.000s into a whole number of bars *and* a
  whole number of samples per bar (100 BPM gives 25 bars of 115,200 samples;
  120 BPM gives 30 bars of 96,000), so the loop point is a bar line.
- **Seamless by construction, not by crossfade.** Note tails, delay taps and
  the reverb all wrap modulo the loop length (`add_wrapped`, `np.roll`, and
  circular FFT convolution), and every LFO completes a whole number of cycles.
- **There is no scipy here.** Filters are additive: each harmonic gets its own
  time-varying gain from a 4-pole lowpass magnitude response at that harmonic's
  frequency. That is a real moving filter, it is vectorised, and it is
  inherently antialiased because partials above 0.45 x SR are never generated.
- **A per-bar FFT is the cheapest bug detector in this file.** It caught a hard
  octave clamp on the sub bass that was silently rewriting F and E roots to G,
  collapsing two different chords onto one bass note on two of the four tracks.
  Re-run that check after any change to voicing or register.
