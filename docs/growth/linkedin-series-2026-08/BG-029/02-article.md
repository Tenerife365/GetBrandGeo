## Article body

Across four European cities we asked AI engines the same commercial questions twice, once in the local language and once in English. Berlin produced the widest gap: German and English answers overlapped by only 8.9%. Madrid produced the narrowest: 23.2% overlap, nearly triple Berlin's.

On the face of it, Spanish-language AI visibility and English-language AI visibility look more alike in Madrid than they do anywhere else we measured.

They do not. One category is doing almost all of the work.

What we did

Madrid was one of four European cities in a bilingual study we ran on 10 July 2026. Four commercial categories per city, each asked twice, once in the local language and once in English. Four engines answered: Claude, Gemini, Perplexity and Meta AI.

Two disclosures. ChatGPT returned an API error on all 32 prompts that day, so it is absent from this data rather than recorded as a zero. Meta AI was retired from our own product six days after this run, and its answers stay in the dataset, because removing a measurement to match a later product decision would falsify the record.

The category that agreed with itself

We asked, in Spanish, which hotel is recommended near Madrid-Barajas airport, and in English, the identical question. Both languages returned substantially the same hotels: Crowne Plaza Madrid Airport, Hilton Madrid Airport, Hotel Barajas Plaza, Hotel Clement Barajas, Hotel Maydrit Airport, Ibis Aeropuerto Barajas, Meliá Barajas. This was the highest-agreement category in the entire four-city study.

There is nothing Spanish about that result. It is a category made almost entirely of international chains. Their names do not translate. Their websites already exist in both languages. The sources that describe them describe them the same way in either language. There is very little for the language of the question to change.

The categories that did not agree

Restaurants for a business dinner behaved like the rest of Europe. Asked in Spanish, the engines named Amazónico, Ramses, El Invernadero, Maison Umami, Santceloni, La Castilla. Asked in English, they named Botín, Coque, DSTAgE, Saddle, Dani Brasserie, El Pichón, La Marisquería. Four names appeared in both: DiverXo, Kabuki, El Prior and La Tasquita de Enfrente.

Boutique hotels split the same way, and produced the single cleanest case in the Madrid data. Only YOU Boutique Hotel Madrid was named by two separate engines when the question was asked in English, and by no engine at all when it was asked in Spanish. It is a real, well known Madrid hotel. Its own brand name is in English.

Real estate agencies split by size rather than by fame. Spanish returned Gilmar, ÔKAM, Monago Consultores, Neinor Homes, Tecnocasa. English added the international networks: Knight Frank, Keller Williams, Remax, Lifetime Properties.

What was removed before counting

Booking.com, Tripadvisor and Google Hotels appeared as competitors in the hotel answers and were removed, because they are booking platforms, not hotels. The Madrid neighbourhoods Salamanca, Retiro and Malasaña were removed from the real estate and restaurant lists. Fotocasa was kept but is a listings portal rather than an agency, a slightly different answer to the question that was asked. A garbled fragment, "Hotel.lr", and one name we could not match to any real Madrid hotel, "The Basic's", were removed.

The rule this gives a Spanish business

Madrid is the clearest demonstration in the study that the size of your language gap is a property of your category, not of your country. If you compete against international chains whose names are the same in every language, both languages will tend to converge on the same list, and an English-language check will roughly describe your Spanish position.

If you are an independent competing against other independents, which is most Spanish businesses in most categories, the two lists diverge, and the Spanish one is the one your customers are reading. In the three non-chain categories here, three quarters of the companies named appeared in only one language.

This is also why "we checked, and we show up fine" is not a complete answer on its own. Showing up fine in the English-language check tells you about the half of the conversation that behaves like the airport hotels: international, already bilingual, already the same list everywhere. It tells you nothing about the half that behaves like the boutique hotel, the restaurant, or the real estate agency, where the language of the question is doing the deciding. We published a nine-item checklist alongside this study, BG-034, covering the site-level details that decide whether an engine can even find your second-language presence in the first place.

Honest limits

Four categories in one city on one day, with four engines answering and ChatGPT absent because it errored throughout. Gemini returned parseable company names in only one of the eight Madrid prompts, so Madrid's comparison rests mainly on Claude, Perplexity and Meta AI. That makes Madrid the thinnest of the four city datasets, and its 23.2% the number in this study we would least want anyone to quote without the caveat attached.

The direction of the finding is not in doubt, though, because it survives on the named cases. A hotel named twice in English and never in Spanish is not a rounding artefact.

The way to know your own number is not to trust ours. Ask both versions of your own question, of at least three engines, on the same day, and compare the two lists yourself.
