  <h2>Worth funding: repeated runs of a fixed question list</h2>

  <p>The one measurement that holds up is running the same questions on the same engines at a regular interval and keeping the text. It is cheap to do by hand and easy to scale later. What you get is direction: are you named more or less often, described more or less accurately, than last time. Write the interval into the plan (monthly is one option; pick one you will actually keep) and treat any single run as a data point, not a result.</p>

  <h2>Worth funding: accuracy of what is said about you</h2>

  <p>Counting mentions is easy. Checking whether the statements are true takes someone's time, and that time is the real cost of this line. It is also the measure with the clearest action attached: a wrong statement points at a page you can fix. If your budget only covers one kind of effort, put it here.</p>

  <h2>Fine as context, not as a target: referral visits from AI products</h2>

  <p>If your analytics shows visits that arrived from an AI product, report them. But do not set a target on them. Many people read an answer and never click, and those who do may land on your home page rather than the page that persuaded them. A rise in referrals is encouraging. A flat line proves nothing. Present the number next to the repeated-run results, never in place of them.</p>

  <h2>Fine as context, not as a target: branded search</h2>

  <p>People who meet your name in an answer may search for it afterwards. That is a reasonable thing to watch in Search Console. It is also affected by advertising, seasons, press and a dozen other causes, so it can support a story but cannot carry one.</p>

  <h2>Leave out: a single score used as a grade</h2>

  <p>A composite score is useful for comparing your own runs over time, if the method stays the same. It is a poor KPI on its own: the same prompt can return different brands on different days (see <a href="/bg-015.html">BG-015</a>), so a score can move without anything having changed, and a score going up says nothing about whether customers noticed. That applies to the baseline score in our own free plan as much as to any other. If a score appears in your plan, name the method and the date range next to it.</p>

  <h2>Leave out: a promised rank or share as a target</h2>

  <p>Targets like "first in the list for our category by June" assume the lists are stable. They are not. A plan that promises a position invites a January argument about a number nobody controls. Report share of voice as a trend instead, as <a href="/bg-084.html">BG-084</a> suggests.</p>

  <h2>Leave out: revenue attributed to AI answers</h2>

  <p>Unless you have a clean way to ask new customers how they found you and a habit of recording the answer, do not put an AI revenue figure in the plan. A short question on the order form or the first call ("did an AI assistant help you find us?") is cheap and gives you something real to quote later. It will undercount, and you should say so.</p>

We will report direction, not rank, and we will not attribute revenue." That is a line a finance lead can approve and an auditor can check.</p>
