Can a Business Game an AI Recommendation?

A paper at EMNLP 2026 tested 22 ways of rewriting web content to push AI shopping agents toward flawed products. It worked, by as much as 83 percentage points, and simple defences only partly undid it. What that means for the line between making true facts easy to find and manipulating an agent.

Most of what is written about generative engine optimization, GEO, assumes the goal is fair: help an AI engine find and understand the facts about your business. A paper presented at EMNLP 2026 in October asked the uncomfortable version of the question. If content is rewritten to push an AI agent toward a product, can it push the agent toward a bad one?

The answer, in the study's setting, was yes. And the defences the researchers tried only partly fixed it.

What we read, and what it cannot tell you

The paper is "SafeGEO: Understanding Generative Engine Optimization Risks in Recommendation Agents", in the main proceedings of EMNLP 2026, held in Budapest. We read its listing and abstract on 6 November 2026 and report only what those state. We have not reproduced the experiments, and we do not describe the individual attack types, because the abstract does not. The study is about products and recommendation agents. It did not test local service firms or live answers from ChatGPT, Gemini or any other public engine.

What the study measured

The setting is an AI recommendation agent: a system that reads sources about products and picks a set to recommend to a user. The researchers rewrote the sources using generative engine optimization, meaning content changed specifically to gain visibility in AI systems, and checked whether the agent still made choices that served the user.

Two things in that table deserve a second look. First, "up to" means the largest effect across the attacks and cases, not the typical one. Second, the target was a flawed product, one that should not have been recommended. The study was not measuring whether optimization helps a good product get noticed. It was measuring whether it can make an agent recommend something that is worse for the user.

Two kinds of optimization

The study draws a line that anyone working on AI visibility should keep in view. On one side is making true facts easy to find: your services, prices, location, credentials, written plainly where an engine can read them. On the other is writing content whose purpose is to change the engine's choice regardless of whether you are the better choice.

Our own studies of 591 AI answers to local buyer questions show what engines actually repeat about local firms. In BG-112, 329 answers stated at least one plain fact about a firm, most often how long it had been in business. In BG-108, 186 answers quoted a star rating or review count, and 86% of the ratings quoted were 4.5 or higher. Those are the facts that end up in front of a buyer. The honest work is making sure they are true and current.

The SafeGEO result is a reminder of what the other side looks like at its strongest: in a test setting, content written to steer an agent moved its picks a long way.

Why manipulation is a risk to the business that does it

The study already tests the agent's side: simple defences cut harmful promotion by as much as 39.2 points. Our reading, not the paper's: research like this is published so that defences get built, and content written to game today's agent is aimed at a target that is being studied. A tactic that works now can stop working, or start counting against you, without notice.

There is also the buyer. A local firm recommended on the strength of claims it cannot back up meets the buyer in person. In BrightLocal's 2026 consumer survey, 97% of people who used AI for local recommendations said they sometimes double check them against real reviews. A recommendation won by steering is one the buyer may check and reject.

How this fits with earlier GEO research

The paper most GEO advice still rests on is the 2023 Princeton study, which tested nine content changes on its own benchmark, GEO-bench, and found a measured gain for five of them, including adding statistics and adding quotations. We went through it technique by technique in BG-082. SafeGEO looks at the same lever from the other side: if content changes can raise visibility, they can raise it for the wrong product too. Both studies run in controlled settings, and neither tells you what a given public engine will do with your page tomorrow.

The gap between a product agent and a local business is narrowing. In a post of 17 November 2025, Google said AI Mode can check real-time availability across booking partners for restaurant reservations, event tickets and beauty and wellness appointments, then hand the buyer a short list. That is an agent choosing among local businesses. The questions SafeGEO asks about products will be asked about those choices too.

What to do with this

Put your effort into facts that are true and easy to verify: the year you opened, what you charge, where you are, what you are certified in. State them the same way on your site and your directory profiles. That is the kind of optimization nobody needs to defend against.

Be wary of any service that promises to change what AI engines recommend through content written for the engine rather than the buyer. The SafeGEO result says such tactics can work in a lab. It also shows defences against them already being tested.
