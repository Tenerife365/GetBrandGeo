AI Visibility in Austin: Three Engines Agree on Home Builders, Not Lawyers

We asked ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini six local buyer questions about Austin. Across the six, only 16 firms were named by all three engines, and 12 of them were home builders. No personal injury firm, real estate agent or startup law firm made all three lists.

Ask three AI engines the same question about Austin and you get three lists. We wanted to know how much those lists overlap, because a business that appears on only one of them is invisible to buyers who use the other two.

On November 6, 2026 we put six local buyer questions about Austin to ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini: a personal injury law firm, real estate agents, a property management company, an immigration lawyer, a technology startup law firm and a home builder. Then we counted the firms that all three engines named.

How this was measured, and what it cannot tell you

18 answers: six questions, three engines (ChatGPT on gpt-5.5, Claude on claude-sonnet-4-6, Gemini on gemini-2.5-flash), all collected on November 6, 2026. We took every name an engine put in bold, then read each of the three answers to the same question to see whether it named that firm anywhere, in bold or in running text. Headings and advice labels were removed. Firms named after a person are reported as a count, not by name.

An exact match on bold text alone finds only 6 firms on all three lists, because engines shorten names and Claude grouped several builders in one line. The hand read finds 16. This is one day of answers. It does not rank anyone and it does not say why an engine chose a firm.

Three of the six questions produced no firm in common at all. Each engine named several personal injury firms, agents and startup lawyers; the lists did not meet. On the startup law question two firms were named by two engines each, never by the third.

Home builders: the one question where the lists overlap

Home builders named20162938 different builders

Of the 38 builders, 12 were named by all three engines, 3 by two and 23 by one engine only. The twelve include Coventry Homes, Highland Homes, Lennar, Meritage Homes and United Built Homes, plus seven builders that carry a founder's or family name. Gemini gave the longest list, 29 builders, and 14 of them appeared in no other engine's answer.

So even here, where agreement is strongest, most builders an Austin buyer could hear about depend on which engine they ask.

Property management and immigration

All three engines named 1836 Property Management and Keyrenter Property Management Austin (Gemini wrote "Keyrenter Austin"). ChatGPT and Claude shared two more property managers that Gemini did not mention.

For immigration lawyers, two practices appeared in all three answers. Both carry their attorneys' names. In one case Gemini named the attorney rather than the firm, while ChatGPT and Claude named the firm, which is a small reminder that an engine may know you by a person's name and not by your brand.

Why counting bold names is not enough

Most AI visibility tools, ours included, start by reading the names an engine puts in bold. On these 18 answers that alone finds 6 firms on all three lists; reading the answers finds 16. The gap comes from how engines write names. Claude put "Lennar & D.R. Horton" in a single bold line and did the same with five more builders in two other lines, so a tool reading one name per bold line misses six builders that Claude did name. Gemini wrote "Keyrenter Austin" where the other two wrote "Keyrenter Property Management Austin". And in one immigration answer the firm appears only through its attorney's name.

For a business owner checking their own visibility, the lesson is simple: search the full answer for your name and its variants, not only the highlighted list.

ChatGPT bolded the most, but not every bold span is a firm: 21 of its 164 contain a digit (a rating, a review count or a number inside a label), and others are advice labels. Claude's stored answers stop at about 3,800 characters, so its length is a lower bound. Gemini's longest answer was the home builder one, where it named 29 builders.

What does not show up

No personal injury firm made all three lists, and no real estate agent did either. Each engine named several firms or agents for both questions; the names did not repeat from one engine to the next. For a firm in those categories, being on one engine's list says little about the other two. BG-016 looks at when engines agree in general.

What an Austin business should do

Ask each engine your own buyer question, by name of your trade and "Austin", and note which list you are on. Treat the three as separate channels: in five of six categories here, no firm was on all of them.

Check how each engine writes your name. If one engine knows you by a founder's name, a shortened brand or an old firm name, make the same name appear on your site, your directory profiles and your Google Business Profile, so every engine can match it to you.
