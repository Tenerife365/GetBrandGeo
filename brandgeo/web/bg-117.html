How AI Engines Use AILA When They Recommend Immigration Lawyers

We read 97 AI answers about immigration lawyers in 20 US cities. ChatGPT named the American Immigration Lawyers Association in all 19 of its answers, mostly as a way to check a lawyer. Perplexity named it in none of 20.

Ask an AI engine for the best immigration lawyer in your city and one name comes up that is not a law firm: the American Immigration Lawyers Association, AILA, the national association of immigration attorneys. We counted how often each engine names it and, reading every answer by hand, what it uses the name for.

43 of 97 immigration answers named AILA. None of the 488 answers to our other questions did, so this is a signal specific to the trade. The engines split into two habits. ChatGPT named AILA in all 19 of its answers and, in every one, told the buyer to use it to check or find a lawyer. Claude named it in 13 of 20, and every time as a membership attached to a lawyer it was recommending. Perplexity never named it.

How this was measured, and what it cannot tell you

We asked the same immigration lawyer question in each of 20 US cities. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity and Google AI Mode answered on July 24 and 25, 2026, and 97 usable answers came back (ChatGPT 19, after one city's July rows were removed in a later refresh). We searched the answer text for "AILA" as a whole word and for the full name, then read all 43 matching answers and sorted each mention into one of two uses: a credential attached to a named lawyer or firm, or advice to the buyer to use AILA to check or find a lawyer. An answer can do both.

Stored Claude answers are cut at about 3,800 characters and ChatGPT and Google AI Mode answers at 10,000, so the counts are lower bounds for those engines. We count what the engines wrote. We did not check whether the lawyers named are AILA members, and we cannot say why an engine named the association or whether doing so affects which lawyers it picks.

12 answers used AILA both ways, 11 of them from ChatGPT. So 21 answers used it only as a credential and 10 only as a check.

AILA as a credential

In 33 answers the association appears inside a lawyer's or firm's description: a member, a past chapter chair, a speaker at its conferences, a firm whose attorneys all belong. This is the use Claude, Gemini and Google AI Mode share. Claude did it in all 13 of its AILA answers, often several times in one answer, one line per recommended lawyer, next to bar admissions and languages spoken. Gemini did it in 7 of its 8. Google AI Mode did it twice, once under a heading it labelled "Credentials".

AILA is one of several credentials in these answers. Board certification or a certified specialist title, which BG-112 counted across trades, appeared in 29 of the 97 immigration answers: Claude 9, ChatGPT 7, Google AI Mode 7, Gemini 6, Perplexity 0. ChatGPT often put the two side by side, telling buyers to prefer a lawyer who is an AILA member or board certified in immigration law.

AILA as a way to check a lawyer

In 22 answers AILA was addressed to the buyer, not attached to a firm. Here ChatGPT is almost alone: 19 of the 22. Its advice took three forms, often more than one per answer:

The other engines did this once each. One Gemini answer called the AILA lawyer finder the most important resource and suggested finding smaller firms through it. One Claude answer, after its list, told the buyer to look for AILA members. One Google AI Mode answer carried it in its source list, through a university page that sends students to the association's site.

This sits next to what BG-111 found across all trades: ChatGPT is the engine most likely to tell a buyer to verify a firm before hiring it. In immigration answers, AILA is the specific check it names.

Why Perplexity's zero needs context

Perplexity's 20 immigration answers averaged about 1,100 characters, against 3,000 to 3,900 for ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini. Two of them said Perplexity had no search results to work from. None mentioned board certification either, and one mentioned a bar association. Perplexity's short answers carried almost no credentials of any kind, so its zero for AILA is part of that pattern, not a sign it treats the association differently from other credentials.

For context, the government bodies also came up: USCIS, the federal agency that decides most applications, in 22 answers (ChatGPT 12, Google AI Mode 5, Claude 3, Gemini 1, Perplexity 1), and EOIR, which runs the immigration courts, in 3, all from ChatGPT. The professional association was named about twice as often as the agency.

What this means for an immigration firm

Two different buyers see two different things. A buyer who asks Claude, Gemini or Google AI Mode sees AILA as a line in some lawyers' descriptions and not in others. A buyer who asks ChatGPT is told, in most answers, to ask every lawyer about AILA membership or to look them up through the association. Either way, membership is the one credential in this trade that four of the five engines brought up unprompted.

What to do with this

Ask the five engines who the best immigration lawyers in your city are, and note whether your membership, chapter role or board certification appears next to your name, and next to your competitors'. If ChatGPT tells a buyer to check AILA, make sure your listing there is current and matches your website.

On your own site, state each attorney's AILA membership and any chapter role in plain text on their profile page, with the year, alongside bar admissions and certifications. This study cannot show that doing so changes which lawyers an engine names. It shows how often the engines bring the association up, and how differently they use it.
