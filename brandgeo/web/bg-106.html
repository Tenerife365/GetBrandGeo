The Directories AI Engines Name When They Recommend Local Professionals

We read 591 AI answers to local buyer questions across 20 US cities. Two in three named at least one third-party platform: a lawyer directory, a review site, a real estate portal. Which ones depends almost entirely on the profession.

When someone asks an AI engine for a personal injury lawyer in Houston or a property manager in Phoenix, the answer rarely stops at a list of firms. It tends to explain why those firms, and the explanation often leans on someone else: a lawyer rated by Super Lawyers, an agent ranked on Zillow, a manager with an A+ from the Better Business Bureau. We wanted to know how often that happens, and which platforms the engines reach for.

In 591 answers across 20 US cities, 390 named at least one third-party platform. That is 66%, two answers in three. Which platform depended almost entirely on the profession being asked about.

How this was measured, and what it cannot tell you

We asked six local buyer questions in each of 20 US cities: a personal injury lawyer, real estate agents, a property management company, an immigration lawyer, and two questions specific to that city's economy, such as entertainment lawyers in Los Angeles or craft breweries in San Diego. Five engines answered each one: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity and Google AI Mode. Collection ran on July 24 and 25, 2026, and 591 answers came back usable.

We then searched each answer's text for 30 platform names. Where an engine's stored answer includes its source links, a link to the platform counts as a mention. A mention is not a ranking factor: it tells you what the engine said, not how it chose. We dropped U.S. News from the count because a plain text match could not tell its legal rankings apart from unrelated uses of the word "news".

Three patterns worth noticing

Legal directories travel as credentials. Super Lawyers appeared in more answers than any other platform we counted, and most of those mentions read as a credential attached to a named firm, in the form "recognized by Super Lawyers", rather than as advice to go and search the directory. Avvo and Justia worked more like places to look. For an immigration question, Avvo appeared in 53 of 98 answers and the American Immigration Lawyers Association in 44.

Property management is judged on reviews. No single directory owns it. Expertise.com, the BBB, Google reviews and Yelp each appeared in 21 to 28 of the 99 answers. Expertise.com leaned heavily on one engine: 57 of its 72 mentions came from ChatGPT.

Real estate agents are filtered through portals. Zillow appeared in 39 of 98 agent answers, and agent-ranking sites such as RealTrends, FastExpert and HomeLight filled most of the rest. Many of these answers recommend individual agents rather than brokerages, with the portal supplying the evidence.

What to do with this

Find your category in the tables above and check your profile on each platform the engines name for it. A lawyer's first stops are Super Lawyers, Best Lawyers, Avvo and Justia, plus AILA for immigration work. An agent's are Zillow and the agent-ranking sites. A property manager's are Expertise.com, the BBB, Google reviews and Yelp.

Check that the facts match your own website: name, address, practice areas, years in business. This study cannot promise that a better profile gets you recommended. It does show that these are the pages engines quote when they explain a recommendation, so an outdated one is the one most likely to be repeated back to a buyer.
