What 45 GEO Studies Agree On, and Where the Evidence Is Thin

A July 2026 survey on arXiv reviewed 45 studies of generative engine optimization published since November 2023. Its verdict is sober: some levers hold up inside an AI answer, almost nothing is shown to make a business easier to find over time. A reader's guide.

Generative engine optimization, GEO, has gone from one academic paper in late 2023 to a whole industry of tools, agencies and advice. The research has grown too, and in July 2026 a survey posted on arXiv read it critically. It reviews 45 studies published between November 2023 and July 2026.

Its conclusion is more careful than most of what is sold under the GEO label, and worth knowing before you pay for any of it.

What we read, and what it cannot tell you

The survey is "Optimizing Visibility in Generative Engines: A Critical Survey of Generative Engine Optimization (2023-2026)", arXiv 2607.14035, submitted on 15 July 2026. arXiv is a preprint server, so posting there is not the same as passing peer review. We read the abstract page on 6 November 2026 and report only what it states. We did not read the full text, so where the survey argues something in detail, we give its conclusion, not its reasoning.

GEO is not one problem, it is a chain

The survey's main argument is about framing. Most advice treats GEO as a ranking problem: do X and you rise in the AI answer. The survey describes it instead as a chain of uncertain steps that cannot be fully observed from outside. The table below condenses it to six steps; the abstract names a few more, including prominence, factual absorption and fidelity.

StepThe question at that step
Search activationDoes the engine search the web for this question at all?
CrawlingHas it fetched your page?
RetrievalIs your page among the sources pulled for this question?
RerankingWhere does it land among those sources?
CitationIs it cited, or used, in the answer?
User behaviourDoes anyone act on it?

Each step can fail on its own, and a study that tests one step says little about the others. To keep the steps apart, the survey proposes what it calls a visibility vector: separate measures for discoverability, citation, absorption into the answer, and economic outcome, instead of one visibility score.

For a local firm the chain is a useful map of what is and is not in your hands. Whether an engine searches the web for a question, and how it ranks what it finds, you cannot control. Whether your pages exist, can be fetched, and state the facts a buyer asks about in plain words, you can. Most honest GEO work sits in that second group.

What it finds well supported

Relevance and position. Once content is in the engine's context, topical relevance and where the content sits in that context are the most reproducible levers across the studies reviewed.

Changing how retrieved content is used. Content that has already been retrieved can causally change how it is cited or used in the answer. That is a real effect, but it begins after the hardest step, getting retrieved.

What it finds thin

Generic tricks. Heuristics that work in one setting transfer poorly to others.

Competition. When many sources apply the same optimization, individual gains can erode.

Citation-first rewriting. Rewriting content to be cited can hurt its chance of being retrieved in the first place.

Lasting effects. The sharpest line in the abstract: no reviewed technique shows a stable, long-term, cross-platform effect on organic discoverability or on what users do next.

A second look at the Princeton paper

The 2023 Princeton paper started the field and is still widely cited as proof that GEO works. The survey's reading is that its gains hold within its own experimental setup, where a source is already in a fixed context, and do not show that content becomes more discoverable or drives lasting traffic. We reached a similar caveat when we went through the paper's nine techniques in BG-082: real effects on a benchmark built for the study, not proof of what a live engine does with your page.

What the commercial audits show

The survey also looks at audits run by companies, not only academic experiments. Its summary: low source overlap, substantial variability from one run to the next, and persistent gaps in factual fidelity, meaning answers that get facts wrong.

That matches what we see in our own measurements. The same local buyer question, asked of five engines, gets very different kinds of answer. In BG-107, four engines named a firm in every answer while Perplexity named none in 15 of 120. In BG-016 we looked at how often engines agree on the same firm. Low overlap and run-to-run variation are the normal state, not an error in the measurement.

What the survey offers instead

Besides the review, the survey proposes a formal multistage model of the chain above, an evidence hierarchy for judging GEO claims, and a protocol for making GEO studies reproducible. Those are tools for researchers more than for a business owner, but the evidence hierarchy is a useful habit: ask what kind of evidence sits behind any GEO claim you are sold. A controlled experiment on a benchmark, a vendor case study and a single screenshot are not the same thing.

What to do with this

Be sceptical of any GEO promise of a lasting lift across all engines. This review of 45 studies did not find one.

Put your effort where the evidence is strongest: be clearly relevant to the questions your buyers actually ask, and make sure the facts about you are accurate wherever engines read them. Then measure what engines say, engine by engine and more than once, because a single answer is one draw from a variable process.
