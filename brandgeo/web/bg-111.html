How Often AI Engines Tell Buyers to Check a Firm First

We read 591 AI answers to local buyer questions across 20 US cities for advice on vetting the firms they name. ChatGPT gave that advice in 83 of its 120 answers. Google AI Mode gave it in 8.

An AI answer to "who is the best immigration lawyer in my city" does two things. It names firms, and sometimes it tells the buyer what to do before calling any of them: check the license, read the reviews, talk to more than one. That second part is advice about you, written next to your name. We read the same 591 answers from our 20 city study for it.

The engines split sharply. ChatGPT gave the buyer at least one vetting step in 83 of its 120 answers (69%). Gemini did in 29, Claude in 16, Perplexity in 9 and Google AI Mode in 8 of 111. ChatGPT wrote 30 of the 39 answers that told the buyer to check a license or bar record.

How this was measured, and what it cannot tell you

We asked six local buyer questions in each of 20 US cities: a personal injury lawyer, real estate agents for a home buyer, a property management company, an immigration lawyer, and two questions specific to that city's economy. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity and Google AI Mode answered each one on July 24 and 25, 2026, and 591 answers came back usable.

We searched the answer text for four kinds of advice aimed at the buyer: check a license, bar record, discipline history or credential; check or read reviews or references; interview or compare two, three or several firms; and ask the firm set questions. We read samples of every kind by hand and tightened the patterns until descriptive uses ("verified reviews") and phone numbers dropped out. Some false matches remain, mostly where Google AI Mode answers include text from the pages they cite, such as a firm's own "read our reviews". Our stored copy of a Claude answer stops at about 2,500 characters, so advice at the end of a long Claude answer is missed. We report what the engines wrote, not why, and one collection run in July is not a statement about other days.

ChatGPT's typical answer ends with a short checklist. It tells the buyer to ask each lawyer whether they have handled this exact type of case, to ask each property manager for the management fee, leasing fee and repair markup, and to ask each agent how many buyer transactions they closed last year. In 66 of its 120 answers it gave questions like these. In 30 it told the buyer to verify a license or discipline record with the state bar or the licensing board. 14 answers across all engines combined three or more of the four kinds of advice, and 8 of those were ChatGPT.

Gemini's advice is narrower. Its most common line is to interview two or three agents before choosing, which it wrote in 21 answers, most of them about real estate. It told the buyer to check reviews in 14. It almost never listed questions to ask.

Claude, Perplexity and Google AI Mode mostly name firms and stop. When Claude adds advice, it is usually to check reviews on Google, Yelp or Avvo, or to book free consultations. Perplexity's advice often comes in a different setting: in 19 of its answers it said it had no search results to work from, and some of those offered questions to ask in place of names, the pattern we covered in BG-107.

Real estate drew the most advice, because Gemini added its "interview two or three agents" line there as well as ChatGPT's checklist. Immigration drew the most license checks: 18 of the 39 answers that told the buyer to verify a license or bar record were about immigration lawyers. On the four professional questions, ChatGPT gave vetting advice in 70 of its 80 answers. On the city-specific questions, where the buyer asked about a local business sector, every engine gave less.

What the buyer reads next to your name

When ChatGPT names your firm, the same answer often tells the buyer to look up your license, read your recent reviews and put a list of questions to you before deciding. When Google AI Mode names your firm, the buyer usually gets names and descriptions and nothing more. The same firm can be presented as a finished recommendation in one engine and as a candidate to check in another.

What to do with this

Make the checks the answers suggest easy to pass. Keep your license or bar number and your admission details on your site where a buyer can find them. Keep recent reviews on the profiles buyers are sent to. And answer the usual checklist questions in plain words on your site: what cases you handle, how many transactions you closed last year, what you charge for leasing and repairs.

This study cannot say that doing so gets you recommended. It shows what a buyer is told to check after an engine names you, and which engine tells them.
