The Brand Fact Error Rate: We Published the Method Before the Data

Existing tools measure whether an AI engine mentions a brand. This study measures whether what it says is true. The protocol is now on Zenodo with a permanent DOI, fixed and dated before a single query runs. Here is what it commits us to, and why the order matters.

Two earlier pieces on this site circle the same gap. BG-103 gives a hand method for checking whether ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude describe your brand accurately. BG-046 covers the BBC's finding that AI assistants were still wrong on sourcing three times out of ten a year after the first test. Both point at a property that the tooling in this category does not report: not whether an engine names a brand, but whether the things it says about the brand are true.

We have now committed to measuring that property, and we have committed to the method in public before collecting any data.

The record

Constantin Daniel (2026). The Brand Fact Error Rate: Study Protocol, First Edition. Zenodo. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22279050. CC BY 4.0. Author ORCID: 0009-0008-4924-1987. The record contains the protocol only. It contains no results, and it says so.

Why the method goes first

Martinez's July 2026 critical survey of 45 generative engine optimisation studies (arXiv:2607.14035) names run-to-run variability as the field's central credibility problem, and it is not the only weakness a reader can point at. A study whose method appears alongside its results can always be suspected of having chosen the method that made the results look good. A method that is dated on a public record before the data exists cannot be suspected of that. So the protocol went up first, with the sample criteria, the fact types, the scoring rubric and the ground-truth procedure all fixed. When the results arrive, they will be published as a separate record that links back to this one, and any departure from the protocol will be stated rather than absorbed.

Ground truth comes from the brand, not from us

The usual objection to accuracy research is the baseline: who says your version of the truth is right? The protocol removes us from that position. Every fact is checked against the brand's own published pages, snapshotted with a URL and a date before any engine is asked anything. The study does not judge what is true about a company. It checks whether the engine agrees with what the company says about itself. Where a brand's page is ambiguous or silent on a fact, that brand-fact pair is excluded and the exclusion is counted, not guessed around.

What gets checked

Nine fact types, chosen because a company publishes them, a machine can check them, and a buyer acts on them. One more is held in reserve for brands with a documented rename.

Five buckets, not right or wrong

Each answer is scored as correct, stale (true at a documented past date, not true now), fabricated (never true at any date), hedged (the engine declines to state the fact) or absent (the engine will not name the brand at all). The split between stale and fabricated is the part of the design we consider most useful, and we are not aware of prior public reporting that separates the two. They call for different remedies. Stale suggests the brand's own page is not being re-crawled or an older source outranks it. Fabricated means the engine produced the statement without a source, and on-site work alone does not address it.

Three runs, because one is not evidence

Every engine is asked every fact about every brand three times, on non-consecutive days. That separates two failures that single-run studies blur together: an answer that is consistently wrong, and an answer that changes between runs. The second is arguably worse for a buyer, because correcting one page does not fix it. Every headline number in the results will carry a stability band.

What the study will not do

It will not publish per-brand error tables that present a company unfavourably. Results are aggregated by stage, country, engine and fact type, and a named brand appears only where the finding is neutral or favourable to it, or where the brand has agreed. It will not present API output as consumer application behaviour: where an engine's programmatic path differs from what a person sees in the app, the engine is either dropped or the difference is stated. And it will not carry a figure that does not trace to the dataset or to a cited source.

Why correctness, and why now

Correctness is the one property in this category with a legal consequence attached. On 28 May 2026 the Landgericht München I (case 26 O 869/26) held that Google is the direct author of an AI Overview's assertions rather than a neutral intermediary, on the reasoning that an Overview does not list results but composes them. A dataset that quantifies how often such composed statements are false about ordinary companies is the empirical companion to that ruling, and the protocol is designed so that anyone can check it.

What happens next

Data collection runs over roughly a fortnight once engine access and terms are confirmed per engine. The first edition's results, the prompt templates, the scoring rubric and the brand selection criteria will be published together as a second Zenodo record linked to the protocol. The same method then repeats quarterly, so that later editions can report change over time rather than a single snapshot.
